Materials
No required text. Diebold, Forecasting in Economics, Business, Finance and Beyond, and Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice (FPP3), both freely available. R and RStudio required; Posit Cloud offered as an alternative.
Assessment
5 homework sets (50 pts each) · 3 exams (150 each, lowest dropped) · forecasting project in three stages: pitch (50), data report (50), final forecast and evaluation report (350)
Outcomes
Mean 93.3%, median 96.7% · 5 A, 2 A‑, 1 C

What the project asks for

A non-technical memo and an econometric handbook, assessed against seven required elements.

  • Motivation, and the asymmetric cost of a forecast that is too high versus too low
  • Justification of the explanatory variables
  • Modeling and data choices: lag structure, aggregation, differencing, logs
  • Breakpoints where the data-generating process differs
  • An explicit judgment on whether the forecast is credible
  • Out-of-sample results reported as MAPE and RMSE

The VAR rubric in use

150 points across six components, weighted by analytical demand, applied with written component-level feedback rather than a score alone.

  • Data preparation and cleaning, 20 pts
  • Visualization and exploratory analysis, 20
  • Stationarity analysis and transformations, 25
  • VAR selection, estimation and diagnostics, 35
  • Model explanation (IRF and FEVD), 25
  • Five-period forecast and interpretation, 25

04 · Reflective statement

What I am trying to do, and what changed

Goals, changes, and failures. Full statement as PDF.

I want students to be able to do something at the end, not to recognize a term. That shows up as a design choice in every course, and it is where the courses are hardest and where they have most clearly failed.

ECON 301: making the assessment match the claim

The course is required for the major, and the students who take it asynchronously are mostly working seniors: thirteen of the sixteen survey respondents held a job. A course like that easily becomes a semester of multiple-choice practice in a publisher platform. So the midterm is two full case studies rather than a timed exam, and each week puts a concept into a decision before the quiz asks about it.

The class did well: median 90.2%, no grade below C‑, 98% clearing 70% on the final. Critical thinking came back at 4.44 of 5.

The case studies are also where the class did worst. Case Study 1 reached my syllabus standard; Case Study 2 did not, at 67%. The instrument I trust most is the one students met least well. That reads as scaffolding, not ability: the rubric arrives with no graded intermediate step, so a student who misreads the assignment finds out only when it is graded. The eight-week section now applies the rubric to a required outline first.

ECON 707/807: a project, staged

The course is built around a forecasting project in three stages: a pitch, a data-and-model report, and a final forecast written for a non-specialist. Splitting it matters because forecasting is a sequence of judgment calls, and a student who submits only at the end never defends a choice while it is still changeable.

All three MA outcomes cleared the threshold, at 100%, 87.5% and 87.5%. That is not the interesting result. Seven of eight scored above 90% on every measure, so the point allocations are not separating competent from excellent work. And the one student who fell short did so on both project outcomes while meeting the exam standard. The failure mode was disengagement from a multi-stage assignment, not an inability to specify a model. Staging the project earlier makes a stall visible while there is still time to intervene.

What did not work

  • An exam it was rational to skip. ECON 707/807 drops the lowest of three exams, and five of eight recorded a non-attempt on Exam 3, correctly, given the policy. An assessment most students can skip is not an assessment. The eight-week version has two exams, both counted.
  • Platform friction I did not catch fast enough. Connect links on the Canvas dashboard resolved to unpublished modules, and some assignments did not match the chapter under discussion. An asynchronous student cannot route around that. I should have audited links weekly, not at module open.
  • A pacing complaint I take seriously. “Less material to review each week”, from a sixteen-week section, is a warning worth carrying into two eight-week ones. Fall ECON 301 clears a full week for the midterm; ECON 625 states the weekly hour cost before add/drop.

Where this is going

Three pieces of work next: convert the ECON 707 project checklists into weighted rubrics; get item-level Canvas data for the ECON 301 measures, written against ten final-exam questions I cannot currently isolate; and finish the eight-week ECON 707/807 build, whose course site is already standing.

06 · Format redesign

Two sixteen-week courses, rebuilt for eight

Both courses were halved in calendar length while keeping their learning outcomes. The sixteen-week and eight-week versions are set side by side below.

What the conversion changed
Course16-week version8-week versionRationale
ECON 301 7 activities, 7 quizzes, 5 homework sets, case study midterm (choose 2 of 3), final. 2,000 pts. 6 activities, 6 homework sets, single-case midterm, comprehensive final. 1,800 pts. Quizzes dropped rather than compressed. They duplicated what the activities already assessed, and cutting them bought back a week of student time without losing coverage.
ECON 707/807 5 homework sets, 3 exams with lowest dropped, project in 3 stages due weeks 10, 13 and 15. Weekly in-person meeting. 5 homework sets, midterm on Modules 1–3 plus a comprehensive final, project staged at weeks 3, 5 and 7. Fully asynchronous. Spatial modeling dropped; cointegration and VAR/IRF kept, because they carry the program outcomes. The project moves earlier so a stalled student is visible before the midterm.

Decisions the eight-week format required

  • Which topics carry the learning outcomes and which are enrichment
  • Where graded checkpoints move so that intervention remains possible
  • Which policies (dropped scores, unlimited attempts) no longer work without recovery time
  • How the weekly hour cost is stated before the add/drop deadline

Written into both syllabi

Both eight-week syllabi state the workload explicitly, 15 to 18 hours per week, and both state that modules stay open so a student may work ahead but may not fall behind.

08 · Program assessment

Course design tied to program outcomes

ECON 707/807 carries three MA program outcomes. I proposed the measures, applied them, and reported the results.

Measures as proposed
OutcomeArtifactRubric weighting
1 · Research and Data Analytical Skills Exam questions requiring estimation and interpretation of a multi-equation/VAR system, scored under the course's best-two-of-three policy Formulation and estimation 30% · identification and model issues 40% · interpretation and economic significance 30%
2 · Critical Thinking and Problem-Solving Forecasting project parts 1 and 2: the pitch and the data/model report Application of time-series methods 40% · depth on limitations and adjustments 40% · quality of forecasts 20%
3 · Communication Skills Final forecasting project and evaluation report, written for a non-specialist decision-maker Clarity on complex results 40% · visuals and language 40% · policy relevance 20%

Two changes I proposed to the measures

Write the drop-lowest policy into the measure language. The draft framework ties outcome 1 to a single designated exam question. Because the course drops the lowest of three exams, a single-exam measure understates performance for students who sit only two, and five of eight students recorded a non-attempt the gradebook correctly excludes. The measure should name the exam score under the drop-lowest policy so the artifact is unambiguous in the next cycle.

Convert the project checklists into weighted rubrics. The forecasting project is currently assessed against a list of required elements with a single point total. The terminal VAR analysis already uses a weighted 150-point rubric; extending that structure to the project is the change I intend to make before the next run.
